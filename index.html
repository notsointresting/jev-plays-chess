How Jev picks a move

Jev is a decision model, not a chat model. It never writes text and never “thinks out loud.” Given a state and a set of typed questions, it reads the answer probabilities straight out of its own internal representation in a single parallel forward pass — no token-by-token generation, no search tree. That's why it answers in tens of milliseconds and can't produce an illegal move.

1

The position becomes a question

Each turn, the app sends Jev the board (FEN + a plain-text diagram) as the state, and one typed Choice question whose options are every legal move. Because the answer must be one of the options we hand it, an illegal move is impossible by construction — not merely unlikely.

2

The code does the counting, not Jev

Jev is fast and knowledgeable but does no lookahead and is weak at arithmetic. So before asking, the app resolves each capture sequence in ordinary code (cheapest attacker first, played to the end) and writes the result into every move's description — “…but then the opponent wins 3 points, a serious blunder”, or “delivering checkmate”. Jev chooses among options that already state their consequences.

3

One parallel readout, calibrated

Jev reads a probability for every legal move at once and returns them together, each trained against real outcomes (a method TypeSafe calls RLCD). The app plays the move Jev believes in most and paints the whole probability distribution as heat on the board — so you see not just what it played but how sure it was and what it nearly played instead.

4

A second question rates the position

In the same request, a typed Score question asks “who's better?” on a five-level scale. Jev answers it in parallel — it can't read the move question — and that drives the eval bar beside the board.

The honest part: Jev plays like someone who knows chess but calculates nothing — it decides in one glance. The tactical warnings it reads come from the app's code, not from Jev searching ahead.
